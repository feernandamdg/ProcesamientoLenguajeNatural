Artificial intelligence is a branch of computer science that studies how to create systems capable of analyzing information, recognizing patterns, and making decisions from data. In recent years, this field has become increasingly present in everyday life because it appears in search engines, virtual assistants, recommendation systems, automatic translators, educational applications, and text analysis tools. Although many people associate artificial intelligence with robots or futuristic machines, much of its real operation happens inside programs that process large amounts of information and generate useful responses for different tasks.

One of the most important fields within artificial intelligence is natural language processing. This discipline aims to help computers work with human language, whether written or spoken. To achieve this goal, systems must identify words, phrases, grammatical structures, approximate meanings, and relationships between terms. Human language is complex because the same word can have several meanings, a sentence can depend on context, and spelling mistakes can change the interpretation of a message. Despite these difficulties, modern models can classify texts, detect opinions, summarize documents, answer questions, and extract relevant information.

Ngrams are a classical technique used to represent texts through sequences of characters or words. A character ngram takes consecutive fragments from a word or sentence. For example, a word can be divided into small blocks that preserve clues about its shape, endings, and combinations of letters. This technique is useful for language detection because each language has frequent patterns. In English, combinations such as the, ing, ion, and, com, ent, tion, and ers often appear in many texts. In other languages, different patterns appear, even when the subject of the text is similar.

To build a language detector, reference texts written in each language must first be collected. After that, those texts are cleaned to reduce unnecessary differences. Usually, they are converted to lowercase, line breaks are removed, and the use of spaces is controlled. Then the ngrams are generated and their frequencies are counted. With these counts, a profile is created for each language. That profile works like a statistical fingerprint of the text because it shows which fragments appear most frequently.

When a new text arrives, the program performs the same process. It cleans the content, generates its ngrams, calculates its profile, and compares it with the reference profiles. Classification is performed by measuring the distance between profiles. If the profile of the new text is closer to the English profile, then the system classifies it as English. If it is closer to the Spanish or French profile, the result changes according to the smallest distance found. This approach does not need to understand the full meaning of the text; it only needs to analyze the distribution of fragments.

One advantage of working with character ngrams is that the system can recognize a language even when new words appear that were not present in the training texts. This happens because unknown words still contain letter combinations that are typical of a language. For example, a long English word can include endings, consonant patterns, or structures that resemble other words from the same language. In this way, the detector does not depend only on memorizing exact words, but on comparing general patterns.

This method is simple, but it can be surprisingly useful as a first approach for language classification, document analysis, and preprocessing in natural language processing projects. It also helps students understand how a text can be transformed into measurable data. Instead of treating language as something abstract, the program converts it into lists, dictionaries, frequencies, rankings, and distances. With those elements, a computer can make a decision that appears intelligent, even though it is based on a clear statistical procedure.
